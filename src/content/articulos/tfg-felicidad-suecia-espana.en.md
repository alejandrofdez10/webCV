## Abstract

This thesis tackles a deceptively simple question with deep socioeconomic implications: are Swedes objectively happier than Spaniards, and if so, why?

Starting from **World Happiness Report** (UN) data — where Spain and Sweden occupy markedly different positions — the work dissects the mechanisms behind these differences, combining objective indicators with cultural and philosophical dimensions.

---

## Theoretical Framework

### Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs
Maslow's pyramid serves as a structural framework: sustained happiness requires meeting basic needs (economic security, health, housing) before self-actualization becomes reachable. Sweden presents stronger indicators at the foundational levels.

### Easterlin's Paradox
**Easterlin's Paradox** establishes that beyond a certain income threshold, economic growth stops translating into greater happiness. The analysis shows that both Spain and Sweden exceed this threshold, shifting the explanation toward qualitative factors — wealth distribution, institutional quality, and social trust.

### Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions
Hofstede's model reveals deep differences between the two countries:

- **Individualism**: Sweden scores very high (71), favoring personal autonomy supported by a strong welfare state.
- **Power distance**: Spain scores higher, reflecting more rigid hierarchical structures.
- **Masculinity/Femininity**: Sweden is one of the world's most "feminine" countries (5/100), prioritizing work-life balance, equality, and cooperation over competition.
- **Uncertainty avoidance**: Spain scores considerably higher, translating into greater anxiety in ambiguous situations.

### Gross National Happiness (GNH) and the Bhutan Model
Drawing on Bhutan's approach, the thesis questions whether GDP per capita is sufficient to measure social progress, or whether composite indicators of subjective well-being, environmental sustainability, and cultural cohesion are more relevant.

---

## Spain vs. Sweden: Key Comparisons

### Economic Indicators
- **GDP per capita**: Sweden (~$55,000) exceeds Spain (~$32,000), though both are above Easterlin's threshold.
- **Gini index**: Sweden has a more equal income distribution (0.27 vs. ~0.33 for Spain), reducing perceived injustice and improving collective well-being.
- **Unemployment rate**: Spain historically doubles or triples Sweden's rate, especially among youth — generating vital insecurity and lowering average happiness.

### Welfare State and Public Services
| Dimension | Sweden | Spain |
|---|---|---|
| Social spending (% GDP) | ~30% | ~24% |
| Life expectancy | 83 years | 84 years |
| Parental leave | 480 shared days | 16 weeks |
| Universal health coverage | Yes | Yes |
| Free public education | Through university | Through high school |

### Institutional Trust and Social Capital
Interpersonal trust and trust in institutions is a robust predictor of happiness. Sweden leads European rankings in institutional trust; Spain shows significantly lower levels, partly due to perceptions of corruption and recent economic crises.

## The Cultural Factor: Lagom vs. Mediterranean

### The Swedish Concept of *Lagom*
*Lagom* (pronounced /ˈlɑːɡɔm/) is a Swedish term without a direct translation meaning roughly "the right amount" or "not too much, not too little." It is a cultural principle that permeates Swedish life:

- Work-life balance (shorter working weeks, longer vacations)
- Social egalitarianism: success is not flaunted ostentatiously
- Moderate and sustainable consumption patterns
- Conflict resolution through consensus

### Mediterranean Style and Relational Well-Being
Against Swedish individualism, Spain excels in relational dimensions of happiness frequently overlooked by quantitative indices:

- **Strong social bonds**: denser, more active family and friendship networks
- **Street culture and shared leisure**: public space as a source of well-being
- **Gastronomy and daily rituals** as sources of hedonic enjoyment
- **Resilience**: greater emotional resilience and ability to put things in perspective

The thesis argues these factors explain why Spaniards report life satisfaction levels higher than their objective indicators would predict.

## Conclusions

1. **The differences are real but nuanced**: Sweden outperforms Spain on nearly all objective WHR indicators (GDP per capita, income distribution, social support, perceived freedom). However, the gap is smaller than these figures suggest.

2. **Easterlin's Paradox operates in both cases**: beyond a certain income level, additional material improvements yield diminishing returns on subjective well-being.

3. **Happiness is multidimensional**: indices based solely on GDP or per capita income are insufficient. Social trust, culture, the nature of affective bonds, and a sense of belonging matter as much — or more.

4. **Public policies with real impact**: reducing youth unemployment, improving institutional trust, and strengthening the welfare state are the levers with the greatest potential to improve well-being in Spain.

5. **The Swedish model is not uncritically exportable**: cultural, historical, and climatic differences make a direct transposition unviable; Spain must build its own well-being model by leveraging its relational and Mediterranean strengths.
